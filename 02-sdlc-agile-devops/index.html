Section 1 of 22

SDLC phases

7 concepts · about 40 minutes

The software development life cycle (SDLC) is the set of stages every piece of software passes through, from an idea to retirement. Every team has one, written down or not. DevOps does not remove these stages. It shortens the loops between them and automates the hand-offs.

Goals
  • Name the phases of the software development life cycle and what each one produces
  • Explain why cost of change rises the later a defect is found
  • Map each phase to the DevOps practices and tools that support it
1.1

Why a life cycle exists

In plain words

Building software without stages means building before you know what is wanted, testing after it is too late, and releasing without a plan. A life cycle gives work a shape: decide, build, check, release, run.

The problem it solves

Unstructured work leads to rework. Studies and experience agree that fixing a defect found in production costs many times more than fixing the same defect when it was a line in a requirement.

What it does

  • Breaks a large uncertain job into smaller steps with clear outputs.
  • Creates points where someone can say stop, change or go.
  • Gives managers, engineers and auditors a shared vocabulary.
  • Applies at any size: a one-line fix and a new platform both pass through the same stages, only the duration differs.

Cost of change

pythonIllustrative relative cost of fixing one defect
# Commonly cited orders of magnitude, not exact measurements
stages = {"requirements": 1, "design": 5, "coding": 10, "testing": 20, "production": 100}
for name, cost in stages.items():
    print(f"{name:<13}{'#' * (cost // 5 or 1):<22}x{cost}")
Check yourself
Why is a defect cheaper to fix early?

Fewer people, artifacts and customers depend on the wrong decision, so less has to be undone.

1.2

Planning and requirements

In plain words

Planning decides whether to do the work and what the outcome should be. Requirements describe what the software must do (functional) and how well it must do it (non-functional).

The problem it solves

Teams often build the wrong thing very well. Vague requests like "make it faster" cannot be tested or estimated.

What it does

  • Captures functional needs (a user can pay by card) and non-functional needs (99.9 percent availability, p95 latency under 300 ms, data stays in the EU).
  • Identifies stakeholders: users, owners, security, operations, compliance.
  • Produces a scope, success measures, risks and a rough budget.
  • Operations requirements, such as monitoring and recovery time, belong here too. Raising them late is a classic source of friction.

Turning a vague wish into a testable requirement

textVague versus testable
Vague      The checkout should be fast and reliable.
Testable   95 percent of checkout requests finish in under 400 ms over a 30 day window.
Testable   The checkout service is available 99.9 percent of minutes per month.
Testable   Card numbers are never written to logs. A CI check scans for 16 digit patterns.
Check yourself
Give a non-functional requirement for a login API.

For example, p99 latency under 500 ms and 99.95 percent availability, measured monthly.

1.3

Design and architecture

In plain words

Design decides how the system will meet the requirements: components, data, interfaces, hosting and security. Architecture is the set of design decisions that are expensive to change later.

The problem it solves

Without a design, each developer makes private decisions and the pieces do not fit. Without recorded reasons, nobody remembers why a database or cloud region was chosen.

What it does

  • High level design sets components and boundaries; low level design details each API, table and algorithm.
  • Considers constraints: team skills, budget, compliance, existing platforms such as Kubernetes or AWS.
  • Records big decisions as Architecture Decision Records (ADRs): context, decision, consequences.
  • Includes threat modelling and capacity estimates before code exists.

A short ADR

textdocs/adr/0007-use-postgres.md
# 7. Use PostgreSQL for orders
Status   Accepted, 2026-10-12
Context  Orders need transactions and reporting joins. The team knows SQL.
Decision Use Amazon RDS for PostgreSQL 16, Multi-AZ.
Consequences Strong consistency. Higher cost than DynamoDB at low volume. Schema migrations needed in CI.
Check yourself
What goes into an ADR besides the decision?

The context that forced the choice and the consequences, good and bad.

1.4

Implementation

In plain words

Implementation is writing, reviewing and integrating code. In a modern flow it includes the build, tests and review as part of the same activity.

The problem it solves

Large changes written alone for weeks collide with everyone else's work and hide defects until the end.

What it does

  • Small changes in short-lived branches, reviewed by another person.
  • Coding standards, linters and formatters enforced by tools rather than by arguing in reviews.
  • Infrastructure is code too: Terraform, Helm and Kubernetes manifests are versioned, reviewed and tested.
  • Continuous integration builds and tests every push, so a broken change shows within minutes.

Local checks before pushing

bashA repeatable pre-push routine
git switch -c feature/order-status          # one small purpose per branch
git add -p                                  # review your own diff in pieces
git commit -m "Add order status endpoint"
git diff --stat origin/main...HEAD          # how big is this change?
Check yourself
Why keep pull requests small?

They are reviewed faster and more carefully, merge with fewer conflicts and are easy to roll back.

1.5

Testing and verification

In plain words

Testing checks that the software meets the requirements and does not break what already worked. It happens at several levels and, ideally, on every change.

The problem it solves

Manual testing at the end is slow, inconsistent and the first thing cut when time is short. Releases then carry unknown risk.

What it does

  • Unit tests check one function; integration tests check parts together; end-to-end tests check a user journey; non-functional tests check load, security and resilience.
  • Verification asks "did we build it right" (against the spec). Validation asks "did we build the right thing" (for the user).
  • Automated tests run in the pipeline; exploratory testing by people finds what scripts do not expect.
  • Test environments should resemble production in versions and configuration.

A test as a gate

bashFail the build when tests fail
set -e
echo "running unit tests"; true       # replace with: pytest -q   or   go test ./...
echo "running linter"; true           # replace with: ruff check .
echo "gate passed"
Check yourself
Which is validation, a unit test or a user acceptance session?

The user acceptance session, because it checks the product against the real need.

1.6

Deployment and release

In plain words

Deployment puts a build into an environment. Release makes its features available to users. They can happen at different times.

The problem it solves

Manual releases at midnight with a long checklist are error prone and stressful, so teams release rarely, making each release bigger and riskier.

What it does

  • Automated pipelines build one artifact, once, and promote the same artifact through environments.
  • Strategies such as rolling, blue-green and canary reduce risk; feature flags separate deploy from release.
  • Every release has a rollback or roll-forward path and a way to tell if it worked (health checks, metrics).
  • Changes are recorded for audit: who deployed what, when, from which commit.

Promotion by digest

bashDeploy the exact tested image
IMAGE=registry.example.com/shop/api
DIGEST=sha256:3f1c0e9d5a7b            # shortened example; use the full digest in real use
echo "kubectl set image deployment/api api=${IMAGE}@${DIGEST} -n staging"
echo "kubectl rollout status deployment/api -n staging --timeout=120s"
Check yourself
Why promote the same artifact instead of rebuilding per environment?

A rebuild can produce different bytes, so what you tested is no longer what you ship.

1.7

Operations, maintenance and retirement

In plain words

After release the software runs, is monitored, patched, extended and eventually retired. This is usually the longest and most expensive phase.

The problem it solves

Treating release as the finish line leaves no one owning reliability, security patches or the cost of keeping old systems alive.

What it does

  • Corrective maintenance fixes bugs; adaptive keeps pace with platform changes; perfective improves features; preventive reduces future risk.
  • Monitoring, alerting and on-call turn production behaviour into input for the next planning round.
  • Dependencies and base images need regular updates; unmaintained ones become security debt.
  • Retirement means migrating users, archiving data per policy and removing infrastructure so it stops costing money.

Maintenance chores to automate

bashSpot stale dependencies and images
kubectl get pods -A -o jsonpath='{range .items[*]}{.spec.containers[*].image}{"\n"}{end}' 2>/dev/null | sort | uniq -c | sort -rn | head
echo "then compare tags against the latest supported versions and schedule updates"
Check yourself
Name the four kinds of maintenance.

Corrective, adaptive, perfective and preventive.

SDLC artifacts and governance

6 concepts · about 40 minutes

An artifact is anything a phase produces that someone else relies on: a requirements list, a design record, a built image, a test report, a runbook. Good teams keep artifacts few, current and close to the code.

Goals
  • Identify the key documents and records produced in each phase
  • Write user stories with acceptance criteria and a clear definition of done
  • Describe change management, traceability and what auditors expect from a pipeline
2.1

Artifacts by phase

In plain words

Each phase leaves behind evidence and inputs for the next one. Knowing them tells you what "done" means for a phase.

The problem it solves

Hand-offs fail when the receiver does not know what they should get. Missing runbooks, undocumented configuration and unknown test coverage are all missing artifacts.

What it does

  • Planning: business case, scope, risk register, roadmap.
  • Requirements and design: backlog items, acceptance criteria, ADRs, diagrams, threat model, API contracts.
  • Build and test: source, merged pull requests, build logs, test and coverage reports, container images, SBOM.
  • Release and operations: release notes, change record, runbooks, dashboards, alerts, postmortems.

Keep artifacts near the code

bashA repository layout that carries its own artifacts
mkdir -p /tmp/shop/{src,docs/adr,deploy,runbooks,.github/workflows}
touch /tmp/shop/README.md /tmp/shop/docs/adr/0001-record-decisions.md /tmp/shop/runbooks/high-latency.md
find /tmp/shop | sort
Check yourself
Which artifact would you open first at 3 a.m. during an alert?

The runbook linked from that alert.

2.2

User stories and acceptance criteria

In plain words

A user story states a need from the user's side: "As a customer, I want to see my order status, so that I know when it arrives." Acceptance criteria are the checks that prove it is done.

The problem it solves

Long specification documents are read by nobody and go stale. One-line feature requests leave out the conditions that decide whether it works.

What it does

  • A story follows the INVEST ideas: independent, negotiable, valuable, estimable, small, testable.
  • Acceptance criteria are often written as Given / When / Then scenarios, which map directly to automated tests.
  • Stories are a conversation starter, not a contract; details are agreed when the work is picked up.

Example

textStory with acceptance criteria
Story    As a customer I want to see my order status so that I know when it arrives.

Scenario Order shipped
  Given an order with status SHIPPED and a tracking number
  When the customer opens the order page
  Then the page shows "Shipped" and a tracking link

Scenario Unknown order
  Given an order id that does not exist
  When the customer opens the order page
  Then the response is 404 and the page shows a friendly message
Check yourself
Why write criteria as Given, When, Then?

They are concrete, unambiguous and can become automated tests with little translation.

2.3

Definition of ready and definition of done

In plain words

The definition of ready says when a work item is clear enough to start. The definition of done says what must be true before it counts as finished. Both are short team agreements.

The problem it solves

Without them, "done" means "coded" to a developer, "tested" to QA and "live" to the product owner. Unfinished work accumulates silently.

What it does

  • Ready: has a clear goal, acceptance criteria, known dependencies and fits in one iteration.
  • Done: code reviewed and merged, tests pass in CI, security scan clean, documentation and dashboards updated, deployed behind a flag or live.
  • Raise the bar as the team matures; for DevOps teams "done" often means running in production and observed.

A done checklist as a pull request template

markdown.github/pull_request_template.md
## Definition of done
- [ ] Tests added or updated and passing in CI
- [ ] No new critical or high findings in the dependency and image scan
- [ ] Metrics, logs and alerts updated if behaviour changed
- [ ] Runbook or README updated
- [ ] Rollback plan stated for risky changes
Check yourself
Who owns the definition of done?

The whole team, agreed and revisited in retrospectives.

2.4

Traceability from requirement to production

In plain words

Traceability means you can follow a requirement to the code that implements it, the tests that check it and the release that shipped it, and back.

The problem it solves

When an auditor or an incident asks "why does this behave like this" or "which release introduced it", missing links turn a five minute answer into days.

What it does

  • Reference the ticket ID in branch names and commits, for example SHOP-142.
  • Let CI attach the commit, build and image digest to a release record.
  • Use Git tags or release notes generated from merged pull requests.
  • Tools: issue tracker, Git, CI, registry and deployment history together form the chain.

Finding the link

bashNavigate the chain with Git
git log --oneline --grep 'SHOP-142' | head           # commits for a ticket
git tag --contains "$(git rev-parse HEAD)" | head -3   # releases containing a commit
git describe --tags --always                          # nearest tag for the current code
Check yourself
How do you link a commit to a ticket cheaply?

Put the ticket ID in the commit message or branch name and enforce it with a hook or CI check.

2.5

Change management in a pipeline

In plain words

Change management is how an organisation decides, records and approves changes to production. DevOps replaces slow manual boards with automated, evidence-based controls for routine changes.

The problem it solves

A weekly approval board makes every change wait and does not actually lower risk: reviewers see a description, not the evidence. Teams then batch changes, raising risk.

What it does

  • Standard changes (pre-approved, low risk, automated) pass through the pipeline with tests, review and scans as the control.
  • Normal changes (new, higher risk) get peer review and sometimes explicit approval.
  • Emergency changes are fast but reviewed afterwards.
  • The pipeline records who approved, what was tested and what was deployed, giving an audit trail by default.

Protecting production with required approvals

yamlEnvironment protection in GitHub Actions
jobs:
  deploy-prod:
    runs-on: ubuntu-latest
    environment: production        # configured with required reviewers
    steps:
      - run: echo "deploy ${{ github.sha }}"
Check yourself
Why can automated checks beat a change board?

They inspect the actual change on every deployment and leave a record, instead of relying on a description.

2.6

Documentation that stays alive

In plain words

Documentation is useful when it is easy to find, short and updated with the change. Docs separate from the code usually rot.

The problem it solves

Nobody trusts wiki pages that are two years old, so engineers ask people instead, and knowledge leaves with them.

What it does

  • Four kinds, often called Diataxis: tutorials (learn), how-to guides (solve a task), reference (facts) and explanation (why).
  • Docs as code: Markdown in the repository, reviewed in the same pull request as the change.
  • Generate what can be generated: API docs from OpenAPI, diagrams from text (Mermaid), CLI help.
  • Test links and examples in CI.

Link checking in CI

bashFind broken relative links quickly
grep -rhoE '\]\((\./|\.\./)[^)]+\)' docs 2>/dev/null | head -5
echo "use a tool such as lychee or markdown-link-check in CI for the full check"
Check yourself
Where should the runbook for a service live?

In the service's repository, versioned and reviewed with the code it describes.

Section 3 of 22

Plan-driven SDLC models

6 concepts · about 40 minutes

A model is a pattern for ordering the SDLC phases. Early models assume requirements can be known upfront. Newer models accept that learning happens during the work. Knowing the older ones helps you recognise them inside large organisations and regulated projects.

Goals
  • Describe waterfall, V-model, incremental, iterative and spiral models and their trade-offs
  • Explain why long feedback loops cause late surprises
  • Pick a plan-driven model for the situations where it still fits
3.1

Waterfall

In plain words

Waterfall runs the phases once, in order: requirements, design, build, test, deploy, maintain. Each phase finishes and is signed off before the next begins.

The problem it solves

Large engineering projects, such as bridges or hardware, needed order and predictable budgets. Changing a design after concrete is poured is expensive, so planning everything first made sense.

What it does

  • Strong documentation and clear milestones; easy to contract and to report progress.
  • Feedback from real users arrives only at the end, often after months or years.
  • Changing requirements mid-way is hard and expensive.
  • Fits well when requirements are stable and well understood, for example some embedded, safety critical or fixed-scope regulatory work.

Where the risk hides

pythonWhen does the first user feedback arrive?
projects = {"waterfall": 12, "iterative (3 month cycles)": 3, "agile (2 week sprints)": 0.5, "DevOps (daily deploys)": 0.03}
for name, months in projects.items():
    print(f"{name:<28} first feedback after about {months} months")
Check yourself
What is the main weakness of waterfall for software?

User feedback and testing come late, when changes are most costly.

3.2

V-model

In plain words

The V-model is waterfall with testing planned in parallel. Every design phase on the left of the V has a matching test phase on the right.

The problem it solves

Plain waterfall leaves test design to the end. In safety or regulated systems you must show that every requirement was verified.

What it does

4.2

In plain words

Scrum in practice

6 concepts · about 40 minutes

Scrum is the most used agile framework. It is deliberately small: one team, one backlog, fixed-length sprints and three accountabilities. Its value comes from the regular inspection and adaptation, not from the meetings themselves.

Goals

5.1

Scrum roles

In plain words

Scrum has three accountabilities: the Product Owner decides what is most valuable, the Developers (everyone needed to build and ship) decide how, and the Scrum Master helps the team follow the process and removes obstacles.

The problem it solves

When nobody owns priorities, everything is urgent. When managers assign tasks, the team cannot commit to anything. Clear accountability avoids both.

What it does

  • Developers include testers, operations and security skills as needed; the team is cross-functional and self-managing, typically 3 to 9 people.

Kanban and Extreme Programming

6 concepts · about 40 minutes

Kanban manages flow; XP improves engineering. Both fit DevOps well because they favour small batches, fast feedback and sustainable pace.

6.1

Kanban boards and WIP limits

In plain words

A Kanban board shows each work item as a card moving through columns such as To do, In progress, Review, Done. A WIP limit caps how many cards a column may hold.

The problem it solves

When everything is started and little is finished, work waits everywhere and delivery slows. Starting is easy; finishing is the real goal.

What it does

  • Visualise the whole workflow, including waiting and review columns.
  • Limit work in progress; when a column is full, help finish something rather than start more ("stop starting, start finishing").
  • Make policies explicit: what "ready for review" means, how to treat urgent items.
  • Pull work when there is capacity instead of pushing work onto people.

7.1

In plain words

The problem it solves

What it does

  • Velocity (points per sprint) is a planning input for that team only; it must not be compared between teams.

In plain words

Throughput forecasting uses the count of finished items per week from history and runs many simulated futures (Monte Carlo) to give a range of dates with probabilities.

The problem it solves

Estimation meetings cost time and still give single-number answers that are usually wrong. Historical data is free and more reliable.

What it does

  • Needs only a count of items done per period and an item count remaining; slice items to roughly similar size.
  • Produces statements like "85 percent likely to finish by 18 December".
  • Works for Kanban and Scrum teams alike and improves as data accumulates.
  • Re-run it weekly as scope changes.

Run the simulation

pythonMonte Carlo delivery forecast
import random
weekly = [3, 5, 2, 4, 6, 3, 4, 2]       # items finished in past weeks
remaining = 40
results = []
random.seed(7)
for _ in range(10000):
    left, weeks = remaining, 0
    while left > 0:
        left -= random.choice(weekly)
        weeks += 1
    results.append(weeks)
results.sort()
for p in (50, 85, 95):
    print(f"{p}th percentile: {results[int(len(results) * p / 100) - 1]} weeks")
Check yourself
What is the benefit of quoting the 85th percentile?

It states a date you are likely to hit, rather than a date you hit half the time.

DevOps culture and CALMS

6 concepts · about 40 minutes

DevOps is a way of working in which the people who build software and the people who run it share goals, tools and responsibility. It is a cultural change first. Tools such as Kubernetes, Terraform and CI servers make the change practical.

8.1

The wall of confusion

In plain words

Traditionally, developers were rewarded for shipping features and operations for keeping things stable. Each side handed work to the other over a "wall", and each optimised for its own measure.

The problem it solves

What it does

  • DevOps aligns both sides on one goal: fast, safe delivery of value, measured together.
  • Removes hand-offs: the same team builds, deploys, monitors and fixes.
  • Replaces gatekeeping with automation and guardrails.
  • Does not mean "developers do everything". Specialists remain, but they work in the same team or via clear self-service interfaces.

It discourages frequent deploys, so changes batch up and each release becomes large and hard to diagnose.

The Three Ways and value streams

6 concepts · about 40 minutes

The Three Ways, from The Phoenix Project and The DevOps Handbook, summarise why DevOps practices work. Each practice supports speeding up flow, amplifying feedback or building a learning culture.

Goals
  • Explain flow, feedback and continual learning as the Three Ways

9.1

The First Way - flow

In plain words

Make work move from idea to customer faster, in a smooth stream, by making it visible, limiting work in progress and reducing the size of each batch.

The problem it solves

Large batches and long queues delay value and delay learning. Defects in a big release are hard to trace to their source.

What it does

  • Make work visible on a board covering the whole path to production.
  • Limit WIP, shrink batch size, reduce hand-offs and automate repeated steps.
  • Never pass a known defect downstream; build quality in.
  • Never optimise a local step at the cost of the whole flow.

In plain words

The problem it solves

Teams optimise the part they see, such as coding speed, while most elapsed time is spent in queues elsewhere.

What it does

  • For each step record process time (hands-on), wait time (queue) and percent complete and accurate (%C/A).

  • The biggest wait is the first place to improve.
  • Include the non-technical steps: approvals, procurement, change boards.

9.5

Theory of Constraints

In plain words

Every system has one bottleneck that limits its output. Improving anything else does not increase throughput; improving the bottleneck does.

The problem it solves

Teams spend effort speeding up steps that are not the limiting one, so nothing improves at the end of the line.

What it does

  • Five focusing steps: identify the constraint, exploit it (never starve it), subordinate other steps to it, elevate it (add capacity), then repeat because a new constraint appears.
  • In software the constraint is often not coding but review, environments, testing, security approval or deployment.
  • Starting more work upstream of a constraint only builds a queue.

DORA metrics and delivery performance

6 concepts · about 40 minutes

The DevOps Research and Assessment (DORA) programme, now part of Google Cloud, has studied software delivery for years. Its findings, summarised in Accelerate and the yearly State of DevOps reports, show that speed and stability go together rather than trade off.

10.1

The DORA metrics

In plain words

Five measures of software delivery. Throughput: lead time for changes, deployment frequency and failed deployment recovery time. Stability: change failure rate and (in recent reports) rework rate. Older material lists four keys with "time to restore service".

The problem it solves

Without measures, improvement is guesswork. Single numbers such as "number of releases" ignore quality.

What it does

  • Lead time for changes: from commit to running in production.
  • Deployment frequency: how often you deploy to production.
  • Change failure rate: share of deployments that cause a failure needing remediation.
  • Failed deployment recovery time (formerly time to restore service): how long to recover from a failed deployment.
  • Throughput and stability metrics are measured together, per application or service.

7 concepts · about 45 minutes

The safer and smaller a release is, the more often you can do it. This section covers how to push changes out with a limited blast radius and a fast way back.

12.1

Deploy versus release

In plain words

Deploying puts new code on servers. Releasing exposes the new behaviour to users. Separating the two removes most release-day risk.

The problem it solves

When deploy and release are the same event, every deployment is a high-stakes moment and rollback means redeploying.

What it does

  • Deploy often, with new features hidden behind flags or routing rules.
  • Release gradually to a small group, watch metrics, then widen.
  • Rolling back a release becomes switching a flag, which takes seconds.
  • Database changes must be compatible with both old and new code during the transition.

In plain words

  • Rollback with kubectl rollout undo.

Testing strategy and the test pyramid

7 concepts · about 40 minutes

Tests are the safety net that lets you change code quickly. The goal is a suite that is fast, trustworthy and covers risk, not a suite that is big.

Goals

  • Write unit tests and contract tests that give fast reliable feedback

13.1

The test pyramid

In plain words

Have many fast, small tests at the bottom, fewer integration tests in the middle and very few slow end-to-end tests at the top.

The problem it solves

A suite made mostly of browser tests is slow, flaky and hard to debug, so developers stop trusting or running it.

What it does

  • Unit tests: milliseconds, no network or database; run on every save and commit.
  • Integration tests: components with real databases or queues (often in containers); seconds to minutes.
  • End-to-end tests: a few critical user journeys in a deployed environment.
  • Costs rise and feedback slows as you go up; aim for most coverage at the lowest level that can prove the behaviour.

Why not test everything end to end?

It is slow, flaky and gives vague failures, so it is used only for critical journeys.

13.2

Unit tests that last

In plain words

A good unit test checks behaviour through the public interface, runs fast, is independent of other tests and fails for one clear reason.

The problem it solves

What it does

  • Parametrised tests cover many inputs compactly. Property-based testing explores random inputs.
  • Run in any order and in parallel.

Software supply chain, SBOM and quality gates

Modern applications are mostly assembled from other people's code. The software supply chain covers every step from a dependency download to the running container. Attacks such as SolarWinds, Log4Shell and compromised npm packages show why each link needs evidence.

15.1

The supply chain and its attack points

In plain words

Your product depends on source code, libraries, build tools, base images, the CI system and the registry. An attacker can compromise any link.

The problem it solves

Code you never wrote runs with the same privileges as code you did. A poisoned dependency or build step ships to production with your signature on it.

  • Know what you run: an inventory (SBOM) answers "are we affected" within minutes during the next big CVE.

SRE principles, SLIs and SLOs

7 concepts · about 45 minutes

Site Reliability Engineering (SRE) began at Google as a way to run large systems with software engineers. Its central idea is that reliability is a feature with a measurable target, and that the target is not 100 percent.

Goals
  • Explain how site reliability engineering treats operations as a software problem
  • Choose SLIs, set SLOs and compute an error budget
  • Use burn rate alerts instead of raw threshold alerts
16.1

SRE and DevOps

In plain words

DevOps is a set of cultural ideas; SRE is a concrete way to apply them to reliability, with specific practices such as SLOs, error budgets and toil limits. Google describes it as "class SRE implements interface DevOps".

The problem it solves

"Make it reliable" is vague. Without a target, teams either over-invest in reliability that users do not notice, or ship fast and break trust.

What it does

  • Engineers spend at most about 50 percent of time on operational work; the rest goes to automation and engineering that reduce future toil.
  • Reliability is defined from the user's point of view and measured.
  • A shared error budget settles the argument between feature speed and stability.
  • Postmortems are blameless; incidents are managed by clear roles.

Reliability is not 100 percent

Why not aim for 100 percent availability?

It is unreachable (users' own networks fail first), and each extra nine costs far more while slowing feature delivery.

16.2

SLIs - what to measure

In plain words

A service level indicator (SLI) is a measurement of one aspect of service quality, expressed as the proportion of good events out of valid events.

The problem it solves

CPU and memory graphs show machine health, not whether users are happy. A server can be healthy and the checkout broken.

What it does

  • Form: good events / valid events, over a time window, giving a number from 0 to 100 percent.
  • Common SLIs: availability (non-5xx responses), latency (requests faster than a threshold), correctness, freshness (data updated recently), throughput for pipelines.
  • Measure close to the user: load balancer or ingress metrics, or synthetic checks.
  • Pick few SLIs per user journey (checkout, search), not hundreds.

Why is a ratio of good to valid events preferred?

It is easy to read, ranges from 0 to 100 percent and works for any kind of event.

16.3

SLOs and SLAs

In plain words

A service level objective (SLO) is the target for an SLI over a window, for example 99.9 percent of requests succeed over 30 days. An SLA is a contract with penalties, usually looser than the internal SLO.

The problem it solves

Without an agreed target nobody knows when reliability is good enough, so every outage becomes a debate.

What it does

  • Set SLOs from user needs and current performance, then tighten gradually. A good SLO is one that users start to notice when missed.
  • The SLO should be stricter than the SLA so you get a warning before paying penalties.
  • Use rolling windows (28 or 30 days) so one bad day fades.
  • Review quarterly; share on a dashboard that product and engineering both read.

It triggers action before the contractual penalty is reached.

16.4

Error budgets

In plain words

The error budget is the allowed amount of unreliability: 100 percent minus the SLO. A 99.9 percent SLO gives a 0.1 percent budget, about 43 minutes in 30 days.

The problem it solves

Product wants to ship fast, operations wants stability. With no shared number, the loudest voice wins.

What it does

  • While budget remains, ship features and take risks. When it is spent, slow down and prioritise reliability work.
  • Convert to events: with 10 million requests per month a 99.9 percent SLO allows 10 000 failures.
  • Agree the policy in advance with product leadership: for example freeze non-urgent releases when the budget is exhausted.
  • Budget also funds experiments such as chaos tests and risky migrations.

16.5

Burn rate alerting

In plain words

Burn rate says how fast you are consuming the error budget. A burn rate of 1 uses the whole budget exactly at the end of the window; 10 uses it ten times faster.

The problem it solves

Alerting on "error rate above 1 percent for 5 minutes" is noisy for small blips and slow for long, slow burns. Neither tells you how much it matters to the SLO.

What it does

  • Alert on fast burn (page) and slow burn (ticket) using multiple windows.
  • The Google SRE workbook suggests, for a 30 day window: burn rate 14.4 over 1 hour (and 5 minutes) pages, because it spends 2 percent of the budget; burn rate 6 over 6 hours pages; burn rate 1 over 3 days opens a ticket.
  • Two windows (long and short) reduce false alarms and make alerts reset quickly.
  • Each page must be actionable and linked to a runbook.

Reliability depends on people as much as on systems. This section covers how SRE teams limit repetitive work, share on-call fairly and respond to incidents in an organised way.

Postmortems, capacity and reliability practice

6 concepts · about 40 minutes

Incidents are expensive lessons. The postmortem is how the organisation collects the value from that expense. Capacity planning and reliability testing reduce the number of lessons you need.

Goals

18.1

Blameless postmortems

In plain words

A postmortem is a written review after an incident: what happened, why, how we responded and what we will change. It is blameless, so people can describe facts honestly.

The problem it solves

Without a review the same incident repeats with a different person on call. With blame, people hide facts.

What it does

  • Written within a few days for incidents above an agreed severity; reviewed in a meeting with all involved.
  • Sections: summary, impact, timeline, root causes and contributing factors, what went well, what went badly, where we got lucky, action items.
  • Focus on how the system and process allowed the failure, not on individual errors.
  • Publish internally so other teams learn.

18.2

Root cause and contributing factors

In plain words

Incidents rarely have a single cause. Several small weaknesses line up. Analysis looks for all of them rather than stopping at the first "human error".

The problem it solves

Stopping at "someone made a typo" leads to "be more careful", which never works. The next typo has the same effect.

What it does

  • Five whys: keep asking why until you reach a process or design gap.
  • Look at detection, mitigation and prevention separately: why did we not notice sooner, why did recovery take this long, why was it possible.
  • Use categories: change, capacity, dependency, monitoring, process, human factors.
  • Prefer fixes that make the right thing easy and the wrong thing hard (validation, automation, guardrails) over training and warnings.

18.4

Capacity planning

In plain words

Capacity planning makes sure there are enough resources for expected load plus headroom, without paying for large idle surplus.

The problem it solves

Too little capacity causes outages in peaks; too much wastes money. Growth, seasonal events and launches change demand.

What it does

  • Know your limits via load testing: requests per second per pod, database connections, queue throughput.
  • Forecast growth from traffic history and business plans (sales, campaigns).
  • Keep headroom: for example plan so that peak load uses at most 60 to 70 percent of capacity, and so that the loss of one zone is survivable (N+1).
  • Autoscale (HPA, Karpenter, Cluster Autoscaler) handles variance; it does not replace planning for limits such as quotas, IP addresses and database size.

Pods required

pythonSize a deployment for a peak

What does N+1 capacity protect against?

The loss of one unit (a node or zone) while still carrying the peak load.

18.5

Graceful degradation and resilience patterns

In plain words

When part of the system fails or load is too high, a resilient service keeps its core function working and sheds or simplifies the rest.

The problem it solves

A failure in a minor dependency, such as recommendations, should not take down checkout. Retry storms can turn a small failure into a large outage.

What it does

  • Timeouts on every remote call; retries with exponential backoff, jitter and a retry budget.
  • Circuit breakers stop calling a failing dependency; bulkheads isolate resources per dependency.
  • Load shedding and rate limits protect the core; fallbacks return cached or default data.
  • Idempotency keys make retries safe for operations like payments.

Backoff with jitter

pythonExponential backoff with full jitter
import random
random.seed(1)
base, cap = 0.2, 10

Careful

Retrying without limits multiplies load on a struggling service. Combine retries with a budget (for example at most 10 percent extra requests) and circuit breakers.

Check yourself
Why add jitter to retries?

It spreads retries over time, so many clients do not hit the recovering service at the same instant.

18.6

In plain words

Practise failure on purpose, in a controlled way, so recovery steps work when you need them. Disaster recovery (DR) covers losing a whole region or data store.

The problem it solves

Untested failover plans fail. Backups never restored may be corrupt. The first time you try recovery should not be during a real disaster.

What it does

  • Game day: scheduled exercise where a team simulates a failure and follows the runbook.
  • Chaos experiments: hypothesis, small blast radius, abort condition, then observe; tools include Chaos Mesh, Litmus and AWS Fault Injection Service.
  • Define RTO and RPO per service and test them. DR patterns on AWS: backup and restore, pilot light, warm standby, multi-site active-active, each trading cost for recovery speed.
  • Fix what you find, and repeat on a schedule.

19.4

Turning quality into numbers

In plain words

Words like "fast", "reliable" and "secure" cannot be tested. A measurable target names the metric, the level, the load and the time window.

What it does

20.7

What technical debt is and how to measure it

In plain words

Technical debt is the future cost of shortcuts and outdated design. Like financial debt it has principal (work to fix) and interest (extra effort every time you touch the area).

The problem it solves

Ignored, interest compounds: simple changes take weeks, outages repeat, and engineers leave. Treated as a vague complaint, it never gets prioritised.

What it does

  • Types: deliberate or accidental, prudent or reckless (Fowler's quadrant). Examples: no tests, outdated libraries, manual deploy steps, unclear ownership.
  • Make it visible: record debt items with an estimate of cost, the interest they cost per month, and what they block.
  • Signals: lead time for changes rising, change failure rate rising, many hotfixes in one module, build time creeping up, dependency age.
  • Example priority: a flaky test costing 5 developers 10 minutes a day is about 4.2 hours a week (5 x 10 x 5 / 60); fixing it in 1 day pays back in two weeks.

20.8

Paying down debt deliberately

In plain words

Debt is managed, not eliminated. Reserve capacity for it, tie it to goals, and make the improvement boy-scout small where possible.

The problem it solves

Big rewrites fail, and "we will fix it later" never arrives without a mechanism.

What it does

  • Reserve a share of capacity (commonly 15 to 20 percent) for debt and operability work, and protect it in planning.
  • Prefer incremental patterns: the strangler fig (route traffic piece by piece to the new implementation), branch by abstraction, and leaving code cleaner than you found it.
  • Attach debt work to feature work in the same area, and to SLO or DORA goals so its value is visible.
  • Automate guards: dependency update bots (Renovate, Dependabot), lint rules, complexity limits, architecture tests.

21.1

Platform engineering and the internal developer platform

In plain words

An internal developer platform (IDP) is a set of self-service tools and defaults, built by a platform team, that lets developers create, deploy and run services without being infrastructure experts.

The problem it solves

Without a platform each team builds its own pipeline, cluster setup and monitoring. Cognitive load rises, quality varies, and security reviews multiply.

What it does

  • Treat the platform as a product: developers are customers; measure adoption and satisfaction.
  • Offer capabilities through APIs, templates and a portal: create service, provision database, deploy, observe, roll back.
  • The platform team owns reliability of the platform, not of every application.
  • Keep an escape hatch: teams with special needs can step off the path, at their own cost.

Platform as a product

Check yourself
What distinguishes platform engineering from a central ops ticket queue?

Self-service, treated as a product with customers and metrics.

21.2

Golden paths

In plain words

A golden path is the supported, well-lit route for a common task, such as building a web service: the template, pipeline, deployment settings and monitoring already wired together.

The problem it solves

Giving developers total freedom produces dozens of ways to deploy. Forcing one rigid way breeds resentment and shadow IT.

What it does

  • The path is optional but so convenient that most teams choose it. Security, compliance and observability come built in.
  • Contents: repository template, Dockerfile, CI pipeline, Helm chart or Kustomize base, default alerts, runbook skeleton, docs.
  • Version the path. Provide upgrade tooling when it changes.
  • Success measure: how long from "I want a service" to "serving traffic".

21.4

Change management: ITIL versus DevOps

In plain words

Change management controls how changes reach production to limit risk. Traditional ITIL processes use a Change Advisory Board (CAB) that reviews requests. DevOps replaces most manual approval with automated evidence and small, frequent changes.

The problem it solves

Heavy approval processes slow delivery but the research shows they do not reduce failure rates. No process at all causes outages from risky changes.

What it does

  • ITIL 4 classifies changes: standard (pre-approved, low risk), normal (assessed and authorised), emergency. Check the current ITIL guidance for definitions.
  • DevOps approach: make most changes standard by proving safety automatically: tests, canary, automatic rollback, small batch size. Reserve human review for high-risk changes.
  • The DORA research found external change approval boards correlate with slower delivery and no better stability; peer review in the pull request is more effective. Check the current DORA reports.

21.5

Incident command: roles and structure

In plain words

For a serious incident, a small set of defined roles replaces chaos: one person leads, one communicates, others investigate. The model comes from emergency services (Incident Command System).

The problem it solves

Without roles, ten engineers debug the same thing, nobody updates stakeholders, and decisions are made by the loudest voice.

What it does

  • Incident Commander (IC): coordinates, decides, delegates; does not debug. Operations lead: runs the investigation and changes. Communications lead: updates stakeholders and the status page. Scribe: keeps the timeline.
  • Declare early; severity levels (for example SEV1 to SEV4) define who joins and how often updates go out.
  • Hand off explicitly after about 2 hours or when tired.
  • Mitigate first: rollback, failover, disable a feature flag, scale up.

Severity table

SEV3:
  meaning: minor impact, workaround exists
  response: handle in working hours

Should the incident commander debug?

No; the IC coordinates so someone keeps the whole picture.

21.6

Incident communication

In plain words

Good communication is part of the fix. Stakeholders, customers and teammates need regular, honest, plain-language updates, even when the update is "no change".

The problem it solves

Silence breeds duplicate pings, panic and speculation. Technical jargon in customer messages causes confusion.

What it does

  • Single channel: a dedicated chat room and a video bridge; pin the current summary.
  • Update on a fixed cadence that matches severity. Use the template: what we know, impact, what we are doing, next update time.
  • External status page: acknowledge, investigate, identify, monitor, resolve. Do not promise a fix time you cannot guarantee.
  • After resolution send a summary and schedule the blameless postmortem within about five working days.

21.7

On call health and learning from incidents

In plain words

Reliable systems depend on rested people. A sustainable on-call rotation, strong handoffs and a blameless review process turn incidents into improvement.

The problem it solves

Burnt-out responders make worse decisions and leave. Blame teaches people to hide mistakes.

What it does

  • Rotation: at least 6 to 8 people for a primary rotation, follow-the-sun where possible; compensation or time off; shadow shifts for new members.
  • Track pages per shift. Aim for a small number of actionable pages (many teams target under 2 per 12 hour shift; adjust to your context). Remove or tune noisy alerts.
  • Postmortem: timeline, contributing factors, what went well, what went poorly, action items with owners and dates. Focus on systems and processes.
  • Review action item completion monthly; an unfinished action is a future outage.

Metrics from pages

People share honest detail, which reveals system causes that can be fixed.

22.1

The FinOps cycle

In plain words

FinOps is a culture and practice of making cloud spending a shared engineering concern. Engineers, finance and product work from the same data and make trade offs between speed, quality and cost.

The problem it solves

Cloud billing is variable and arrives after the spend. Finance cannot judge technical need, and engineers rarely see the cost of their choices, so waste grows quietly.

What it does

  • Cost is a metric like latency: owned by the team that controls it.
  • The aim is value per dollar, not the smallest bill.

22.2

Cost allocation: tags, accounts and Kubernetes labels

In plain words

Allocation assigns each dollar to a team, product or environment. Without it, the bill is one big number nobody owns.

The problem it solves

"The EKS bill went up 30 percent" cannot be acted on until you know which namespace or team caused it.

What it does

22.3

Rightsizing Kubernetes workloads

In plain words

Rightsizing adjusts CPU and memory requests to what workloads actually need. Over-requesting reserves capacity you pay for but do not use.

The problem it solves

Developers copy generous requests "to be safe". Nodes then show 20 percent real utilisation while the scheduler considers them full.

What it does

  • Compare requests to usage percentiles over weeks (p95 for CPU, near max for memory). The Vertical Pod Autoscaler in recommendation mode, or Goldilocks, suggests values.
  • Memory: set requests close to usage and limit equal to request to avoid OOM surprises. CPU: requests matter for scheduling; limits can cause throttling.
  • Bin pack with the cluster autoscaler or Karpenter, which can consolidate under-used nodes. Pod disruption budgets protect availability during consolidation.
  • Example: 20 nodes at 22 percent average utilisation, target 60 percent, need ceil(20 x 0.22 / 0.60) = 8 nodes, a 60 percent reduction in nodes.

Compute the saving

pythonNode count and monthly cost
import math
nodes, util, target = 20, 0.22, 0.60
needed = math.ceil(nodes * util / target)
rate, hours = 0.192, 730             # example hourly price, check current pricing
print("nodes needed:", needed)
print("before $", round(nodes * rate * hours, 2), "after $", round(needed * rate * hours, 2))
print("saving $", round((nodes - needed) * rate * hours, 2), "per month")

22.4

Commitments, spot and unit economics

In plain words

After removing waste, reduce the price per unit with commitments and cheaper capacity, and track unit economics: cost per order, per active user or per request.

The problem it solves

Total spend rising can be healthy (more customers) or unhealthy (waste). Only a unit metric tells them apart.

What it does

  • Cover the stable baseline with savings plans or reserved capacity; run fault tolerant work on spot with diversified instance types and graceful termination handling.
  • Unit cost = total cost divided by a business measure. If cost rose 20 percent while orders rose 50 percent, cost per order fell by 20 percent (1.20 / 1.50 = 0.80).
  • Review commitment coverage and utilisation monthly; unused commitments are waste.

22.5

Measuring developer experience with SPACE

In plain words

Developer experience (DevEx) is how it feels to build and ship software here: fast feedback, low friction, clear goals. The SPACE framework (Forsgren and colleagues, 2021) says productivity has five dimensions and no single metric captures it.

The problem it solves

Counting lines of code, commits or tickets rewards the wrong behaviour and can be gamed. Leaders still need evidence to prioritise platform work.

What it does

  • Satisfaction and well-being: survey results, burnout risk.
  • Performance: outcomes such as quality and customer impact.
  • Activity: counts of actions such as deploys or pull requests (use carefully, never alone).
  • Communication and collaboration: review turnaround, onboarding time, quality of documentation.
  • Efficiency and flow: waiting time, handoffs, interruptions, time in flow.
  • Use metrics from at least three dimensions, include at least one perceptual measure from surveys, and track teams, not individuals.

Gather a few signals

pythonMedian PR review time from sample data

Never use these metrics to rank individuals. Metrics used as targets get gamed (Goodhart's law).

Systems show what happens, surveys show how it feels and why; both are needed.

22.6

DevEx drivers and relation to DORA

In plain words

Research on developer experience (Noda, Storey, Forsgren, Greiler) groups the main drivers into three: feedback loops, cognitive load and flow state. DORA's four key metrics measure delivery outcomes; SPACE and DevEx explain what helps or hinders the people producing them.

The problem it solves

Improving a delivery metric without understanding friction leads to pressure instead of fixes.

What it does

  • Feedback loops: how long builds, tests, reviews and deploys take. Shorter loops give faster learning. Example: cutting a 20 minute CI to 8 minutes saves 12 minutes on each of 10 runs a day per developer.
  • Cognitive load: how much a developer must hold in mind; reduce with golden paths, good docs and simple abstractions.
  • Flow: protected focus time, fewer interruptions, clear goals.
  • DORA: deployment frequency, lead time for changes, change failure rate, time to restore. Check the current DORA report for the exact definitions and any added metrics.

Value of faster builds

22.8

Case studies and lessons

In plain words

Real incidents and migrations teach more than theory. These three composite scenarios are modelled on common public postmortems and are not about any specific company.

The problem it solves

Teams repeat each other's mistakes because the lessons stay inside the organisations that paid for them.

What it does

  • Case 1, the expired certificate: a wildcard certificate for internal APIs expired on a Saturday. Renewal was manual and owned by someone who left. Lessons: inventory, automation, alert at 21 days, ownership in the catalog.
  • Case 2, the config push: a global configuration change was deployed to every region at once and made all instances reject requests. Lessons: progressive rollout with canaries, config validated like code, fast rollback, and out-of-band access to fix the system that deploys.
  • Case 3, the cost spike: a debug log level left on and a cross-zone chatty service raised the monthly bill by 40 percent before anyone noticed. Lessons: budget alerts, unit cost dashboards, allocation labels, cost in the postmortem template.
  • Common thread: small, frequent, observable changes with automatic rollback beat heroics.
