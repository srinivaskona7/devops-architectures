Section 1 of 20

The architect mindset and decision records

8 concepts · about 55 minutes

An architect does not pick the best technology. An architect picks the least bad trade-off for a specific situation, writes down why, and makes it cheap to change when the situation changes.

Goals
  • Frame every design choice as a trade-off against stated requirements
  • Write a short architecture decision record that a new teammate can follow
  • Separate reversible from irreversible decisions and spend effort accordingly
1.1

Requirements before technology

In plain words

A design starts from what the system must do and how well it must do it, not from a favourite tool. Functional requirements say what. Quality attributes say how well: speed, uptime, security, cost, ease of change.

The problem it solves

Teams that start from a tool find reasons to justify it, and discover missing requirements only after launch.

What it does

  • Capture functional needs as user stories or use cases.
  • Capture quality attributes as measurable statements, such as "95 percent of requests under 300 ms" or "recovery within 1 hour".
  • Capture constraints you cannot change: budget, regulation, existing contracts, team skills, deadline.
  • Rank the quality attributes. You cannot maximise all of them, and the ranking drives every later choice.

A measurable quality attribute

yamlquality-attributes.yaml
availability: 99.9 percent monthly
latency_p95_ms: 300
recovery_time_hours: 1
data_loss_minutes: 15
budget_per_month_usd: 5000
team_skills: [kubernetes, postgres]
Check yourself
Why rank quality attributes?

They conflict, so the ranking decides which trade-off wins.

1.2

Trade-offs and the cost of choices

In plain words

Every option gains something and loses something. The job is to make the exchange visible so people can agree on it.

The problem it solves

Hidden trade-offs turn into surprises: a cheap design that cannot meet its uptime target, or a resilient one nobody can afford.

What it does

  • Common tensions: cost against availability, speed of delivery against flexibility, consistency against latency, control against operational effort.
  • Compare options on the same small set of criteria, not on feature lists.
  • Prefer boring, well-understood technology unless a requirement forces otherwise.
  • Count the total cost: build, run, learn, migrate away.

A small weighted scorecard

Weights add to 10. Scores run 1 to 5. Multiply and add.

Criterion Weight Managed DB Self-run DB
Operations effort 4 5 (20) 2 (8)

Total 10 37 35

The scores are only a conversation aid. Change the weights and the answer changes, which is the point: argue about weights, not about brands.

Check yourself
What does a weighted scorecard make visible?

The criteria and weights behind a choice, so people can disagree openly.

1.3

Reversible and irreversible decisions

In plain words

Some choices are two-way doors that cost little to undo. Others are one-way doors that are expensive to reverse. Spend deliberation in proportion.

The problem it solves

Teams either agonise over trivial reversible choices or rush the ones that lock them in for years.

What it does

  • Reversible: instance size, a library, a dashboard layout. Decide fast, measure, adjust.
  • Hard to reverse: primary data store, cloud provider for the core platform, account and network layout, public API contracts, address ranges.
  • For one-way doors, run a short prototype and review with others.
  • Add seams, such as interfaces and infrastructure as code, that lower the cost of change.

1.4

What an architecture decision record is

In plain words

An ADR is a short document, usually one page, that records one decision: the context, the options, the choice and its consequences. It is kept in version control beside the code.

The problem it solves

Six months later nobody remembers why a choice was made, so people either fear changing it or repeat the same debate.

What it does

  • One decision per record, numbered in order, never deleted. A later record can supersede an earlier one.
  • Status values: proposed, accepted, deprecated, superseded.
  • Written in plain language with the reasons and the trade-offs, including what you gave up.
  • Reviewed like code, through a pull request.

Template

markdowndocs/adr/0007-use-managed-postgres.md
# 7. Use managed PostgreSQL for orders

Status: Accepted (2026-10-31)

## Context
Orders need transactions and reporting. The team has two engineers and no database operator.
Availability target is 99.9 percent. Budget is 800 USD per month for the database.

## Options considered
1. Managed PostgreSQL with a standby in a second zone
2. PostgreSQL on VMs run by the team
3. A document database

## Decision
Option 1.

## Consequences
- Good: backups, patching and failover are handled by the provider.
- Bad: limited extension list, some lock-in to the provider tooling.
- Revisit if monthly cost passes 2000 USD or we need an unsupported extension.
Check yourself
Why keep ADRs in the repository?

They are versioned, reviewed and found next to the code they explain.

1.5

Writing a good ADR

In plain words

A good ADR is short, honest about the downsides, and written for a reader who was not in the room.

The problem it solves

Weak records say "we chose X because it is best", which explains nothing and cannot be challenged.

What it does

  • State the forces: requirements, constraints, deadlines.
  • List real alternatives, including "do nothing".
  • Explain why the rejected options lost, with facts.
  • Name the consequences, both good and bad, and a trigger to revisit.
  • Keep it to one page. Link long analysis rather than pasting it.

Lightweight CLI helper

bashnew-adr.sh
#!/usr/bin/env bash
set -euo pipefail
dir=docs/adr
mkdir -p "$dir"
n=$(printf "%04d" $(( $(ls "$dir" | wc -l) + 1 )))
slug=$(echo "$*" | tr 'A-Z ' 'a-z-')
printf "# %s\n\nStatus: Proposed\n\n## Context\n\n## Options considered\n\n## Decision\n\n## Consequences\n" "$*" > "$dir/$n-$slug.md"
echo "created $dir/$n-$slug.md"
Check yourself
What triggers a review of an old ADR?

A condition written in the record, such as a cost or scale threshold, or a changed requirement.

1.6

Documenting the design: C4 and views

In plain words

Different readers need different pictures. The C4 model draws the same system at four zoom levels: context, containers, components, code.

The problem it solves

One huge diagram confuses everyone, and no diagram means each person carries a different mental model.

What it does

  • Context: the system and the people and systems around it.
  • Containers: deployable units such as a web app, an API, a database.
  • Components: major building blocks inside one container.
  • Code: usually generated, rarely drawn.
  • Add a deployment view for cloud layout and a data flow view for security reviews.
  • Keep diagrams as text (for example Mermaid) so they are reviewed and versioned.

1.7

Fitness functions and guardrails

In plain words

A fitness function is an automated check that fails when the architecture drifts from what you decided.

The problem it solves

Decisions written in a document are forgotten. Rules that are not checked decay.

What it does

  • Examples: no public storage buckets, no resource without an owner tag, no dependency from the web layer to the database layer, response time budget in the test suite.
  • Run them in the pipeline and as policy in the cloud.
  • Turn each accepted ADR into a check where possible.

Example policy check

pythoncheck_tags.py
import json, sys

REQUIRED = {"owner", "env", "cost-centre"}
plan = json.load(open(sys.argv[1]))
bad = []
for rc in plan.get("resource_changes", []):
    after = (rc.get("change") or {}).get("after") or {}
    if "tags" in after and not REQUIRED <= set(after["tags"] or {}):
        bad.append(rc["address"])
if bad:
    print("missing required tags:", *bad, sep="\n  ")
    sys.exit(1)
Check yourself
Why automate architecture rules?

People forget documents, but a failing pipeline cannot be ignored.

1.8

Running the architecture review

In plain words

A review is a structured conversation where peers test a proposal against its requirements before money is spent.

The problem it solves

Reviews that are late, hostile or vague either block delivery or catch nothing.

What it does

  • Review early, when changing course is cheap, and again before launch.
  • Send the one-page summary and ADRs beforehand.
  • Walk the quality attributes in order: security, reliability, performance, cost, operations.
  • Record questions and decisions. Assign owners and dates for open risks.
  • Aim to improve the design, not to judge the author.

Section 2 of 20

Well-Architected pillars across clouds

8 concepts · about 55 minutes

All three major clouds publish an architecture framework. The wording differs, but they ask the same questions. Use them as checklists, not as rules to follow blindly.

Goals
  • Name the pillars of the AWS, Azure and Google Cloud architecture frameworks
  • Apply each pillar as a set of review questions to a real design
  • Run a lightweight review and record the risks found
2.1

Why frameworks exist

In plain words

A framework is a distilled list of good practice and review questions based on many customer designs.

The problem it solves

Every team repeats the same avoidable mistakes: single points of failure, wide permissions, no cost visibility.

What it does

  • Gives a shared vocabulary for reviews.
  • Turns "is this good?" into specific questions.
  • Provides tools: AWS Well-Architected Tool, Azure Well-Architected Review, Google Cloud Architecture Framework. Check each vendor's site for the current pillar list, because it is revised from time to time.
Check yourself
Are the frameworks laws?

No, they are review aids, and you may accept a risk knowingly if you record it.

2.2

The pillars side by side

In plain words

The three frameworks overlap strongly. AWS lists six pillars, Azure five, and Google Cloud a similar set under slightly different names.

The problem it solves

When a team uses two clouds, they need to map the vocabulary so reviews are consistent.

What it does

  • AWS pillars (current as of writing): operational excellence, security, reliability, performance efficiency, cost optimization, sustainability.
  • Azure pillars: reliability, security, cost optimization, operational excellence, performance efficiency.
  • Google Cloud: operational excellence, security and compliance, reliability, cost optimization, performance optimization. Sustainability and AI topics also appear. Verify the current lists.

2.3

Operational excellence

In plain words

Operational excellence means you can run the system, see what it is doing, change it safely and learn from failures.

The problem it solves

Without it, deployments are scary, incidents repeat, and knowledge sits in one person's head.

What it does

  • Everything as code: infrastructure, configuration, dashboards.
  • Small, frequent, reversible changes through a pipeline.
  • Observability: metrics, logs, traces and alerts tied to service goals.
  • Runbooks and game days. Blameless post-incident reviews with tracked actions.

Review questions

yamlops-questions.yaml
- Can we rebuild the environment from code?
- Can we roll back a release in minutes?
- Which alert wakes someone and what do they do?
- When did we last practise an incident?
Check yourself
What makes a change safe?

It is small, tested, reversible and observed after release.

2.4

Security

In plain words

Security protects data and systems through identity, least privilege, encryption, detection and response.

The problem it solves

One leaked key or open storage bucket can expose all customer data.

What it does

  • Strong identity: single sign-on, MFA, short-lived credentials, no shared accounts.
  • Least privilege and separation of duties across accounts.
  • Encrypt in transit and at rest, with managed keys.
  • Network layers: private subnets, restricted ingress, private endpoints.
  • Detect and respond: audit logs, threat detection, tested incident process.

Review questions

yamlsecurity-questions.yaml
- Are there any long-lived access keys?
- Can any data store be reached from the internet?
- Who can read production data and why?
- Where are audit logs stored and who can change them?
Check yourself
Why prefer workload identity to stored keys?

Credentials are short-lived and cannot leak from files.

2.5

Reliability

In plain words

Reliability is the ability to keep working correctly and to recover quickly when parts fail.

The problem it solves

Hardware, networks, deployments and people fail. A design that assumes otherwise fails in production.

What it does

  • Remove single points of failure: multiple zones, health checks, autoscaling.
  • Limit blast radius with cells, separate accounts and gradual rollouts.
  • Retries with backoff and jitter, timeouts, circuit breakers, idempotent operations.
  • Backups, tested restores, and defined recovery objectives.
  • Quotas checked in advance, including in the recovery region.

Retry with backoff and jitter

pythonretry.py
import random
import time


def call_with_retry(fn, attempts=5, base=0.2, cap=5.0):
    for i in range(attempts):
        try:
            return fn()
        except Exception:
            if i == attempts - 1:
                raise
            delay = min(cap, base * (2 ** i))
            time.sleep(random.uniform(0, delay))
Check yourself
Why add jitter to retries?

It spreads retries in time so many clients do not hammer a recovering service together.

2.6

Performance efficiency

In plain words

Performance efficiency means using resources well to meet latency and throughput goals, and keeping that true as demand changes.

The problem it solves

Over-sized systems waste money, and under-sized ones frustrate users.

What it does

  • Pick resource types that fit the workload: memory, CPU, GPU, storage class.
  • Measure with load tests against targets such as p95 latency.
  • Cache, use CDNs, and place data near users.
  • Scale horizontally with autoscaling. Remove bottlenecks one at a time, starting with the biggest.

Arithmetic of capacity

At peak, 9000 requests per second. One node serves 1500 requests per second. Nodes needed: 9000 / 1500 = 6. To survive losing one of three zones with two thirds of capacity remaining, run 6 x 3 / 2 = 9 nodes, three per zone. The cost of zone tolerance here is 3 extra nodes, which is 50 percent more.

Check yourself
Why change one thing at a time?

Otherwise you cannot tell which change improved or hurt performance.

2.7

Cost optimization and sustainability

In plain words

Cost optimization means paying only for value you use. Sustainability means doing the same work with less energy and fewer resources, which usually also lowers cost.

The problem it solves

Cloud makes it easy to start resources and forget them, so costs creep up.

What it does

  • Visibility: tags or labels, budgets and alerts per team.
  • Right-size and schedule non-production off outside working hours.
  • Use commitment discounts for steady load and spot or preemptible capacity for tolerant work.
  • Choose efficient hardware, regions with cleaner energy where policy allows, and delete unused data.

Working example, scheduling

A test VM costs 0.10 per hour in an example price. Always on for 730 hours per month is 0.10 x 730 = 73.00. Running 10 hours on 22 working days is 220 hours, which is 0.10 x 220 = 22.00. The saving is 73.00 minus 22.00 = 51.00, about 70 percent. Prices are examples; check current rates.

Check yourself
Which non-production saving needs no code change?

Scheduling the environment off outside working hours.

2.8

Running a lightweight review

In plain words

A review is a short workshop that walks the pillars, records risks, and agrees actions.

The problem it solves

Full formal reviews are skipped because they feel heavy, so risks go unseen.

What it does

  • Choose one workload and invite the people who run it.
  • Spend about ten minutes per pillar, using the vendor question lists.
  • Classify findings as high, medium or low risk, with an owner and date.
  • Repeat when the design changes or every six to twelve months.

6.1

In plain words

Availability is the share of time a service works. It is quoted as a percentage, often called nines.

The problem it solves

Saying "highly available" means nothing without a number everyone can check.

What it does

  • A year has 365.25 x 24 x 60 = 525,960 minutes.
  • Targets are a business decision, with cost rising steeply per extra nine.

6.2

Composite availability

In plain words

A system with several parts in a chain is only as available as the product of the parts. Redundant parts in parallel improve it.

The problem it solves

Teams assume a 99.99 percent load balancer gives a 99.99 percent application.

What it does

  • In series: multiply. 0.9999 x 0.999 x 0.9995 = 0.99840065, so 99.84 percent, which is (1 - 0.99840065) x 525,960 = about 841 minutes a year, 14 hours.
  • In parallel with independent failures: 1 - (1 - a)^n. Two copies at 99.9 percent give 1 - 0.001 x 0.001 = 0.999999, which is 99.9999 percent in theory.
  • Real failures are not fully independent: shared deployments, shared configuration and shared dependencies cap the gain.

6.3

In plain words

RTO is how long you can be down. RPO is how much data you can afford to lose, measured in time.

The problem it solves

Without these two numbers nobody can choose a backup schedule or a recovery design.

What it does

  • RTO (recovery time objective): maximum acceptable time from failure to service restored.
  • RPO (recovery point objective): maximum acceptable age of the last recoverable data.
  • RPO is driven by backup or replication interval. RTO is driven by how much must be rebuilt and how well it is automated.
  • Actual recovery time must be measured by drills.

Worked example

Backups every 15 minutes with log shipping every 5 minutes: worst-case data loss is 5 minutes, so RPO = 5 minutes. Recovery steps: detect and decide 10 minutes, restore database 30 minutes, redeploy and verify app 15 minutes. RTO = 10 + 30 + 15 = 55 minutes. If the business requires RTO of 30 minutes, 55 does not meet it, and the restore step must shrink, for example with a warm standby.

What it does
